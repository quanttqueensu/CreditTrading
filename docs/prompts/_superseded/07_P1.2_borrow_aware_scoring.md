### The mechanism

Borrow is a **holding** cost on a short, paid per day for as long as the
position is on. Trading cost is paid per unit traded. The band cut trading
cost by trading less; it cannot touch borrow, because the same names are
held for longer. The only way to reduce borrow is to short different names,
which means the fee must enter the decision **as a return**, not as a
trading cost.

Concretely: a short is worth putting on only if its expected convergence
over the holding period exceeds what it costs to hold. Today the score is
symmetric in sign; a rich muni with a 10% fee and a rich PIMCO fund with a
0.6% fee get identical short weight at identical z.

### The construction

For name $i$ on decision date $t$, let:

- $s_i = -(z_i - \bar z)$ (cross-sectionally demeaned, cheap positive),
- $\sigma_i$ = trailing 252-day daily price-return volatility, ending the
  day before (`ret.rolling(252).std().shift(1)`),
- $H$ = the realised average holding period of the live band policy in
  business days. **Measure it** from `evaluate(band(T, 0.048), R)["hold"]`
  (mean gross over mean daily turnover); do not assume. Expect ~12–16.
- $IC_H$ = the audit's IC at the horizon nearest $H$, interpolated on
  $\sqrt h$ (use the T+1 column; −0.096 at 5d, −0.131 at 21d),
- $f_i$ = the latest borrow fee, annual decimal, from
  `data/cef/cef_borrow.csv`.

Expected trade return over the holding period, Grinold form:
$$\mu_i^{(H)} = |IC_H| \cdot \sigma_i \sqrt{H} \cdot s_i.$$

Holding cost of a short over $H$ days:
$$b_i = f_i \cdot \frac{H}{252}.$$

Net alpha:
$$a_i = \begin{cases} \mu_i^{(H)} & s_i \ge 0 \text{ (long)} \\
\mu_i^{(H)} + b_i & s_i < 0 \text{ (short: less negative)} \end{cases}$$

Then weights are built from $a_i$ exactly as today from $s_i$: demean,
normalise to unit gross, vol-scale, min-weight filter, band. Demeaning
after the adjustment keeps the book dollar-neutral; note in the write-up
that this shifts the pivot, so the adjustment also slightly re-ranks the
long side. Report that shift.

Worked example at today's numbers, $H = 14$, $IC_H \approx 0.115$:

| name | $\sigma_i$ ann | $\sigma_i\sqrt{H}$ | $\mu$ at $s=-1$ | fee | $b_i$ | net at $s=-1$ | net at $s=-2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| HYT | 16.3% | 3.8% | −0.44% | 10.56% | 0.59% | **+0.15%** (do not short) | −0.29% |
| NAD | 11.6% | 2.7% | −0.31% | 9.98% | 0.55% | **+0.24%** | −0.07% |
| NEA | 11.5% | 2.7% | −0.31% | 3.06% | 0.17% | −0.14% | −0.45% |
| PTY | 20.7% | 4.9% | −0.56% | 1.09% | 0.06% | −0.50% | −1.06% |

Verify these with the real inputs; the point is that at one standard
deviation rich, a 10% borrow makes the short worthless, and the current
score cannot see it.

**IC does not drop out.** The subtraction of $b_i$ is in return units, so
$\mu$ must be too. Use the measured $IC_H$; document that if IC were
halved, the cost term would bite twice as hard, and show that sensitivity
(IC × 0.5, × 1, × 1.5) in the note.

Adopt if net@15 − borrow improves over the reference at all three fee
scenarios, gross Sharpe falls by no more than 0.05, and the permuted
control shows no comparable gain. Otherwise record and stop.

Do not tune $H$, $IC_H$, or a scaling on $b_i$. All three are measured. If
the adjusted book is worse, the answer is "the fee is not large enough to
matter at this IC", and that is worth knowing.

### Caveat to carry

short may become more expensive exactly as it becomes more attractive.
